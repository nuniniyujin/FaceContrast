THE IDEA

Face contrast is more than a single number.

Overall tonal separation, eye definition, and fine skin detail contribute differently to how a portrait looks. We measure each component and validate it against human perception.

Abstract

The quality of smartphone portrait photography has become increasingly important to both manufacturers and users. Recent devices use complex computational pipelines to improve facial rendering under varied lighting. These pipelines vary widely across manufacturers, so the same portrait can look noticeably different across smartphones. These differences affect multiple aspects of facial appearance, making perceptual preference difficult to characterize with a single measure. To address this, we propose an interpretable framework that decomposes face contrast into three components: overall facial tonal separation, eye‑region contrast, and micro‑contrast of fine skin details. We formulate dedicated metrics for each component and validate them via pairwise human judgments, demonstrating that our approach correlates more closely with human contrast perception than traditional contrast measures. To support this analysis, we introduce a realistic mannequin‑based dataset designed for systematic facial contrast evaluation, comprising 508 full‑resolution captures from 29 smartphones under diverse lighting, with region‑specific masks and extensive pairwise perceptual annotations. As a downstream analysis, we show that combining the three component‑wise metrics provides a meaningful basis for modeling balanced facial contrast preference in a controlled setting, and we present a preliminary evaluation on a smaller real‑portrait dataset indicating that insights derived from the mannequin study can extend to real images. Our work provides a controlled first step toward defining and evaluating face contrast in portrait imaging.

01 / METHOD

What do we mean by face contrast?

We define three components of contrast and study how their balance relates to portrait preference.

CONTRAST AMOUNT · FACE

Overall contrast

The global tonal separation across the face: how clearly bright and dark regions are separated. It describes facial shading and the transitions that give the face depth.

How strongly are light and shadow separated across the face?

CONTRAST AMOUNT · EYES

Eye contrast

The local lightness separation within the eye region, between dark structures such as the pupil and brighter structures such as the sclera. It describes how distinctly these structures appear.

How clearly do dark and bright structures stand apart within the eyes?

CONTRAST AMOUNT · SKIN DETAIL

Micro-contrast

The visibility of fine facial details, such as skin pores and small local structures. It describes how strongly fine skin texture is rendered in the final portrait.

How visible and pronounced are the fine details of the skin?

PERCEPTUAL PREFERENCE · THE THREE COMPONENTS TOGETHER

Balanced contrast

The perceived harmony among overall tonal separation, eye definition, and fine-detail visibility: whether they form a natural and visually pleasing balance.

Which portrait has the more pleasing balance of facial contrast?

More contrast is not always better. Balanced contrast is a separate preference judgment over the three components, rather than a fourth measure of contrast amount.

How we measure the three components

All three measures use the L* (lightness) channel of CIELAB, normalized to [0, 1] and denoted by L. This separates tonal variation from chromatic variation. Select a component to explore its computation.

FACE REGION

The structure of light and shadow.

Overall contrast captures tonal transitions across the face. Local lightness variation is normalized by the local mean and pooled over five spatial scales, from fine shading to broader facial structure.

cr(x)=σr(x)μr(x)+ε

Here, μr and σr are the local mean and standard deviation of L, the normalized CIELAB L* channel. The response is averaged over the face and across window sizes 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256.

COMPUTATION AT A GLANCE

Five window sizes, one face-wide measure.

16 × 1632 × 3264 × 64128 × 128256 × 256
  1. Measure local tonal variationNormalize local standard deviation by local mean lightness.
  2. Pool within the faceAverage the response over the face mask at each scale.
  3. Combine the scalesAverage the five scale-wise values to obtain overall contrast.
EYE REGION

Small region. Strong visual impact.

Eye contrast measures the separation between dark and bright structures inside the eyes. A single Otsu threshold partitions lightness values from both eye masks into two classes.

Ceye=μbright−μdark

Here, μbright and μdark are class means of L, the normalized CIELAB L* channel, within the merged left and right eye masks.

COMPUTATION AT A GLANCE

SKIN REGION

The visibility of fine facial detail.

Micro-contrast measures fine skin texture while reducing the influence of broad shading. We extract texture in log-lightness space, measure its local energy, and compress strong responses before pooling over the skin.

Cmicro=1|𝒦|∑x∈𝒦Slocal(x)
𝒦={x∈Ωs|q10≤Slocal(x)≤q90}

Here, Ωs is the skin mask. The thresholds q10 and q90 are the 10th and 90th percentiles of Slocal within that mask. Averaging the retained responses gives Cmicro.

COMPUTATION AT A GLANCE

Isolate texture. Measure its visible strength.

  1. Transform lightness
    Llog=log(L+ε)
    L is the CIELAB L* channel normalized to [0, 1]; ε is a small constant for numerical stability.
  2. Isolate fine-scale detail
    Dlog=Gσfine∗Llog−Gσcoarse∗Llog
    σfine=12,σcoarse=32
    Gσ denotes Gaussian smoothing; ∗ denotes convolution.
  3. Measure local RMS energy
    Elocal(x)=1|N(x)|∑y∈N(x)Dlog(y)2
    N(x) is an 11 × 11 neighborhood around pixel x.
  4. Apply perceptual compression
    Slocal(x)=(Elocal(x))0.6
    Finally, retain the central 80% of responses within the skin mask and average them.
↳

From contrast measures to balanced contrast preference. In the controlled mannequin study, a second-order polynomial regressor combines the overall, eye, and micro-contrast metrics to predict separately collected human judgments of balanced contrast.

Pairwise comparisons

From a controlled scene to perceptual scores.

Original captures and contrast-adjusted variants form an 800-image annotation set. Trained image-quality experts completed four separate comparison tasks over 160 hours.

Pairwise judgments are converted to Just Objectionable Difference (JOD) scores. A difference of 1 JOD corresponds to a 75% choice probability for the higher-scoring image.

BEYOND THE LAB

A first step toward real portraits.

A preliminary evaluation uses 444 images across 10 subjects/scenes, with varied facial appearances, poses, and device processing.

The proposed zero-shot metrics achieve SRCC of 0.825 for overall contrast, 0.768 for eye contrast, and 0.731 for micro-contrast, macro-averaged across scenes.

Broader evaluation across identities, skin tones, poses, and capture conditions remains future work.
